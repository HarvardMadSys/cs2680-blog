Throughout this semester, I worked step by step to build an AI agent through these five assignments. I watched an agent, I wrote an agent myself, I made that agent cheaper, I made that agent work on the class's GPUs, We turned that agent into a general agent. With this blog, I want to share what I've learned throughout this journey. Briefly, I can say that the biggest wins I expected were expected to come from more efficient or more clever prompts, but I saw that the optimizations I made on how the agents spend time and how we measure those made the biggest improvements.

Starting with the first assignment, I initially created a web interface that showed every single step of the agent's life for Claude Code. Some of the things I learned from here were that we should look at the prompt rather than the verdict, because in my journey I didn't have pytest downloaded, so the agent couldn't run the tests. Therefore, it's made a copy and ran those, and outputted "verified". The verdict here wasn't important, but rather it was how the agent built it and the prompts. Also, I realized that recordings could be misguiding. A UI that I made according to a logged run got broken because subagents acted differently on the live run, so from here, I saw that the recordings could be faulty and misguiding.

In the second assignment, I rewrote tool calling, error handling, and termination (basically, the whole agent loop from the ground up) and tested it on 10 bug-fix tasks from SWE-bench Pro. The baseline was able to solve one of every two tasks, and mine solved around 5 or 6 from 10. I learned here that termination is very, very, very important. The loop ended the run when the model output anything without doing a tool call. The solution here that I did was I used a keep working nudge, which actually saved two tasks. I also saw that we should first check a task because three out of four of the unsuccessful task texts contradicted the hidden test. In one of them, the agent wrote the correct code, and then it disrupted it to fit the text. From here, I learned that we should first always check the task.

In the third assignment the model was still an API, but now the goal was the same success at lower cost and higher speed. My agent ran five tasks in parallel, chose between a cheap and an expensive model, and stayed under a <span>$</span>10 budget. I learned that a safety margin can be deliberate. My cost estimate was about 7 times the real cost (<span>$</span>0.242 versus <span>$</span>0.033 in one run), and I kept it because running out of budget mid-run would cost far more than a few skipped retries. The expensive model also burned money fast, spending around <span>$</span>3.3 on one task to cut failing tests from 5 to 3, so I limited it. Sometimes the grader is the problem. Every local run showed all tests failing in 0 seconds, and I blamed the agent until I found that macOS lacks the timeout command. Now I check the grader first when results look too fast or uniform. One task also passed 7 of 8 tests in one run and 3 of 8 in the next.

In the fourth assignment we served the model ourselves with SGLang on one RTX PRO 4500 and connected my A3 agent to it. I went from 4 to 8 solved on the 26 hidden tasks.

The key lesson was to measure the server before tuning the agent. Calls first took over a minute because the cache held only about 391,000 tokens, so 25 conversations didn't fit and just 5 to 8% of each prompt was cached. Capping the agent at 12 concurrent requests raised the hit rate to 93 or 94% and cut calls to under a second. Engine tuning backfired, since one change dropped the hit rate to 25% and a bigger one crashed the server after 96 seconds, so I kept the defaults. Thinking made up 77% of output, so I allowed it only at the start, after errors, and every 8th call, which cut average replies from 653 to 194 tokens. Local results were also optimistic, with 9 or 10 of 25 starter tasks solved on my Mac but only 8 of 26 hidden ones.

Now, coming to assignment 5, our aim is to build an agent that does everything, basically. The same agent should both be able to solve Terminal Bench 2.0 and assignment 4's 26 tasks. The results were 36 out of 89 and 7 out of 26. Some of the learnings I got from here were initially that we should be putting a time budget to thinking. Some of the replies were almost 3 minutes of thinking but output nothing. If you put a time budget based on the remaining time, latency decreased by half, and 5 of the 10 failing tasks were able to be solved.

The biggest mistake I made was that a time plan that I tuned according to a benchmark created problems with the other one. The 40-minute cap that I put on Terminal Bench, unfortunately, resulted in 4 tasks being stopped in the middle of solving. 53 minutes of the 4-hour run were used, and results turned out to be 3 out of 26. With a general time plan, on the other hand, we had 7 out of 26.

I also saw that the load changed the result. In local tests, 3 to 6 tasks were open, and in the leaderboard, 26 tasks all shared the same 1 GPU. Also, the monotonic clock in the container was processing slowly, and when my Mac went to sleep, the SSH tunnel turned off, so some of the infrastructure problems could also be seen as agent bugs. Also, I realize that the variance between the runs was huge. Two versions that had the same time plan saw 36 and 30. By comparing, I learned to analytically analyze the differences, especially based on side-by-side and control-task comparisons. This also connects back to my learnings from the assignment 1 where I saw that the agent only checked the tests. It did it on its own and marked those cases as done and with the review step that I added, it solved one more task and didn't lose any control tasks.

Overall, when I look at the whole journey of the five assignments, what I'd do differently would be to start earlier (because the improvements in the assignments were rather about the iterations that I did based on the results I got, rather than writing and building a great architecture). I would also do tests under realistic loads and in all kinds of run types. I'd also view "done" and "verified" as claims, because I saw that they didn't turn out to be realities.
