Before COMPSCI 2680, my usage with Claude Code, I thought at the time well, was extremely naive. After a response, I would very minimally look through the output and, maybe, ask Claude why it decided to go through one path rather than another. Through assignments 2 to assignment 5, I definitely learned that creating an agent that does long context tasks or hits specific tasks needs a lot more attention on the processes that are happening under the hood than just picking up Opus 5.5 and giving it a long prompt. 

One main lesson that I learned was that improving the system needs careful examinations and experiments. Several changes that seemed promising at first didn’t end up helping in specific tasks or overall assignments. Even some basic implementation fixes made a substantial difference. I think with the advancement of these models, it’s easy for someone to assume that there is some sophisticated way of thinking/approach, which in most cases is the complete opposite. 

## Big Lesson 1: Understanding when to keep going or stop

Looking at assignment 2, specifically, one of my earliest lessons that I hit was to really see if the agent’s control loop actually behaved as intended. For assignment 2, using a toy repository I examined the initial harness. The agent was able to solve the problem at iteration three; however, the loop kept going until iteration twenty-eight and wrote seventeen done calls. The harness wasn’t stopping, at all, and the agent was just signaling general completion. This wasted computation was from an ordinary software bug rather than poor model reasoning. This lesson was a brutal pill to swallow as all of this extra work that I thought was contributing to something that would help me with the assignment submission and the surrounding tasks was just going to waste. After revising the harness, the ansible task went from 122 iterations and 5.50 million tokens to 33 iterations and 0.71 million tokens. I remember switching different components all together, so the stopping fix doesn’t necessarily explain that entire improvement. But, the toy example showed me why termination needed more attention. 

Another lesson that I learned was that response endings need to be looked into with care. For a text only response, it might lead to the harness to halt the workflows that they are pursuing even when the agent had not edited or changed anything related to the tasks. For assignment 2, there was a successful mistletoe work that contained four cut off replies (hitting the max-token limits before finishing a tool call/text argument), and a successful dpath run contained eight. These specific examples showed that an interrupted response didn’t necessarily mean the task was beyond the agent’s capabilities. The harness just needed a way to keep going. 

Naively, I hit another mistake where continuing wasn’t the same as succeeding. For assignment 5, there were changes that included cut off recovery and eliminated four ‘gave up’ endings from the local evaluations. From these four tasks, only one subsequently passed; the other three reached their time limits instead. On my Mac with x86 emulation and a subset of 40 tasks from Terminal-Bench 2 running locally, I was able to get solved increased from thirteen to seventeen where there was one run for every configuration. But, recovery and timeout handling changed. The main lesson was that preventing premature termination removes one obstacle without necessarily solving the underlying task. Instead of looking at every recovered execution as a success of some sorts, I would need to really dig deeper and look into what really happened after each recovery. 

## Big Lesson 2: Pushing more resources does not mean better scores

Yes, there were some changes in the prompts that did lead to some improvements. However, these changes did not have a generalizable benefit. For assignment 2, a qutebrowser task failed since there was a missing module import, then it was passed in attempts after a specific prompt change. Meanwhile, protonmail failed in all four attempts across two prompt wordings. Here, I learned that a targeted prompt change can solve a task without the total agent being improved. Seeing these two examples, I learned to avoid interpreting one successful fix as evidence that the whole system had improved.

For assignment 3, I learned that being able to use more tokens and hitting through more attempts at failing tasks doesn’t guarantee better results. Across three expert-first revisions, the task solves decreased from thirteen to eleven to ten out of twenty-six, while cost per solve increased from $0.64 to $0.83 to $0.94. There was lower accuracy and higher cost even when revisions became faster per solve. Cutting resources too aggressively wasn’t a solution either. A reduced-budget version of rev17 in assignment 3 solved only five tasks. Compared with the original attempt of 14, this was a massive decline. This assignment specifically, although was frustrating, taught me to understand the evaluation process between accuracy, cost, and time together. Giving an agent more resources only helps if it uses them efficiently and productively. And it’s not until you truly understand what is happening to know where those resources should be appointed to and taken from and be reallocated, as removing resources could be a big blocker from completing useful work. 

## Big Lesson 3: Pursue smaller changes and testing what matters

One of the larger mistakes that I made was changing too many things at once. For rev12 in assignment 3 I included fifteen different and distinct changes and finished with nine solves out of twenty-six. Not good at all. These results gave me little guidance as well about what changes I should keep to iterate further for the goal of better tasks solved. Another revision, rev14, was able to get five solves when I had forecast with local testing nine or ten. This taught me to have these expectations as hypotheses and iterate on smaller changes that produce interpretable results. 

Assignment 3 and assignment 4 gave me a way to look at different valid comparisons and to optimize measured bottlenecks. In assignment 3, I took out the benchmark-derived rules and separated earlier results from compliant builds. 1,038 minutes was for model requests versus the ten minutes of tool execution, while navigation was 55% of 911 tool calls, showing that decreasing unnecessary interactions when trying to build could help. 

For assignment 4, there was one version that had fourteen of twenty-five tasks solved, but eight of twenty-six on the leaderboard under different conditions. A batching prompt was able to reduce turns by 10% locally where solves stayed at seven of twenty-six at the leaderboard. Being able to use ten workers, other than eight, slowed retries from around 26.5 to thirty-eight seconds for every turn, where the leaderboard task solved went from 17/26 to 16/26. At the same time there was a context token gate that increased throughput in a replay benchmark from 9.5 to 18.1 turns per minute and a cache hit rate from 0.26 to 0.93. This submission has 10/26 tasks solved on the leaderboard in two hours and forty-four minutes. From each iteration, submission, and results, they all showed me why improvements need testing in the target environment. 

One lesson from assignment 5 was to handle output limits carefully and to see beyond the total scores. Tokens after position 2,048 in every reply took up for 46% of all completion tokens throughout 3,016 calls. Showing run-to-run variation, the same code for the agent with cut-off recovery twice on the same 40-task local Terminal-Bench 2 subset gave out 17 tasks solved in around fifty-nine minutes and 15 tasks solved in around 93 minutes.
