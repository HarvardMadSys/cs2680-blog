For Assignment 5, I mostly didn't think much about the task at all, and even prompted very little. Two Claude Code agents ran in a loop on an AWS box and worked on it fully on their own, and I checked in every few days. Now, I am not here to claim that this got me to Rank #1. In fact, it by no means did that, as I got 0 bonus points for Assignment 5, but I think this development process of how I approached the assignments for CS2680 has some valuable lessons I want to share nonetheless.

<!--more-->

## From clipboard to cron

In Assignment 2, I was basically a human clipboard. One Claude chat planned everything and wrote prompts for Claude Code sessions, and I copied them from one window into the other. Claude Code wrote the harness and ran the experiments. I made a lot of decisions and steered the main orchestrating agent in the directions that I thought were valuable, making calls such as which of the given tasks were unsolvable and which harness variants to keep. I think, though, that was still quite a simple setup, but from Assignment 3 onwards it got a lot more interesting!

In Assignment 3, I got tired of being the clipboard. Additionally, the leaderboard rewarded many attempts due to the non-compounding $30 daily limit. Then an idea came to me, because I went on vacation and wanted to continue submitting, yet didn't want to "work" on the project. So I started letting Claude Code run without me.

The setup roughly worked like the following: With a local Claude Code session, I brainstormed a bunch of different ideas of what to potentially implement in Assignment 3 as strategies for my harness. I saved this to a big .md file, which throughout the next two weeks also got updates every 2-3 days based on what I thought should be changed. Now, the core part of the setup was an autopilot on an EC2 machine that ran 27 build-and-review loops, where one session changed the harness, another reviewed the change, and the loop uploaded the result. It made submissions #11 to #37 almost completely on its own. I had a set of predetermined prompts, where one would be selected randomly, that would motivate the planning session to try to either exploit something that had already worked well in the past or explore something entirely new.

Towards the end, I replaced the autopilot with smaller "labs". There, lead sessions each pushed one line of work that I told them to push (faster, cheaper or more solves), and independent reviewer sessions still checked them before anything would actually be uploaded to ensure quality. I quickly noticed that going faster was the lowest-hanging fruit and then simply only pushed in that direction, which secured me a Rank #1 spot. The lab queued submissions #38 to #63. Here, though, my steering the direction of what to actually optimize, i.e. focusing on a few key integrations or one of the different optimizable dimensions, was key, instead of just saying "optimize the harness, make no mistakes".

Since my Assignment 3 loop worked quite well overall, I also created such a loop for Assignment 4, but I basically checked in on the loop way less regularly and gave it less input on which direction to actually optimize. The setup, though, was similar: the Assignment 4 loop ran on its own AWS box from 22 October to 9 November, and cron kicked off three runs a day. Every run had three agents: a planner (Fable 5.1) that picked the next change, an implementer (Opus 5.5) that built it, and a reviewer (Fable 5.1) that had to say GO before anything got uploaded. Additionally, every four to six runs, a persistent Opus orchestrator sat down with a read-only GPT Astra 6 reviewer and rewrote the strategy and the objective. Here I was hoping to better automate the steering of the big picture of what exactly to optimize, using preset prompts that encouraged large changes. However, this did not work well. In the end, with my little intervention and Claude mostly acting on its own, I only got to a Rank #17 position.


For Assignment 5, I tried exploring whether maybe a big error of mine was using a new session for every submission. Thinking about how good the Claude Code harness has also become at compacting context by itself autonomously and keeping what is important over very long chats (though at a higher usage cost, of course), I only used two persistent Claude Code agents on one box: A worker (Opus 5.5) woke up every hour, read its own notes, checked the leaderboard and did whatever was next. A read-only reviewer (Fable 5.2) got a brief from the worker every evening at 20:00 and wrote back, and the worker answered point by point. I never reset either session, so the worker ran on one context for eleven days. My only steering wheel was a text file that I could edit from my local machine and that got glued onto every prompt.
The start was honestly great. Four hours after its first turn, the worker had built a fresh agent, uploaded it, and solved 35 of the 89 TB2 tasks in 2 h 13 min. Baseline met on day one, and I hadn't done much steering, so I was quite stoked that this would work out well without me having to interfere much.

## Assignment 3 - the one that worked

Assignment 3 is the one that went well, so it's worth figuring out what exactly my agent loop did together with my steering. What it converged on was surprisingly simple. The final agent ran five tasks at a time with one attempt per task and no retries. The stronger model (DeepSeek V4 Flash) took basically all tasks, with the Qwen model from Assignment 2 only as the fallback. An edit tool that applies up to 20 edits in one call saved a lot of turns: 16 calls carried 76 edits. Also, only ever appending as the newest message kept 92 to 97% of the input cached. On the way there, the loop had built money shares, model cascades, triage for "hopeless" tasks and a strategy document that reached version 51. The winner was a stripped-down version of all that.

And luck helped. I'd be lying if I left that out. Assignment 3 allowed a run to stop after n confirmed passes, so the board really scored how fast you got to n. The exact same code, run 12 times, solved 8 to 11 tasks at anywhere from 1:31 to 8:42 per solve. This was largely due to the model's randomness, which we obviously just cannot eliminate, but we could still use the variance to our advantage, which is kinda exactly what I did by reuploading the same code, which I knew worked somewhat well, over and over again.

## Assignments 4 and 5: more autonomy, worse results

In the next two assignments, I stepped in less, basically guiding the optimization work of the Claude Code loops less, and as a result of that, I scored a lot worse in those assignments.

In Assignment 4, my crowd of agents approved its own work 44 times out of 44. Then I did one bug sweep using Fable and Ultracode and found 60 problems; a lot of them were, for example, Assignment 3 settings that Claude Code had just left in and that I hadn't looked at myself. I ended at Rank 17, with 44 of 60 points, and no outstanding approaches to talk about for the work that I ended up submitting. This was a failure on my end of simply not stepping in as much and directing the optimization, which led to Claude Code just looping over editing "irrelevant" settings, such as the max number of turns per task, and trying to optimize those numbers instead of working on actual bigger architectural changes in the system.

Assignment 5 was much leaner, and it still got stuck in these local minima. After the baseline, I told the agents to go only for bonus points. From 13 to 18 November the loop graded 12 uploads (#3 to #14), and every single one was a repeat or a settings change: how many tasks run at once, a per-task time budget, context masking, thinking on or off, sampling. TB2 stayed between 27 and 34 the whole time. The one real win was turning thinking off, which took the 26 SWE-bench-Pro-style tasks from 4 solves to 9.

The worker and the reviewer also got along a little too well. The reviewer judged every plan inside the worker's own frame. The worker wrote a response file after each of the 11 reviews. I grepped all of them, and the word "reject" doesn't show up once. In the worker's defense, the brief prompt I wrote called the reviewer "a stronger model", but this basically made it so one Claude Code session (Opus 5.5 medium) just blindly accepted everything the other Claude Code session (Fable 5.2 xhigh) said, without actually creating a system where the agents challenge each other to get to better results.


## What I'd change about the loop

Looking back, the same thing kept happening in every assignment. The agents got stuck polishing small things. They tuned budgets, flipped flags and tried sampling settings that changed the agent's behavior ever so slightly, while really I sat so far behind the Pareto front that no single parameter could close the gap. What I needed were bigger changes to the whole architecture, which the agents just didn't propose at all on their own.

What did help was me telling them. For example, in Assignment 5 at one point I saw other students at 71 of 89 while my agents and I were stuck around 30, and I told my Mac Claude session that the agents were in a local minimum. My local orchestrating session gave the loop new instructions the next day: stop tuning settings, look at how good everyone else is doing, and think big again. Within hours, the worker had found a newer model, Ornith-1.5, and with thinking on, it later pushed TB2 to 37 and then 41, which were then my best counts by far. The reviewer also turned into an actual strategist for a few days and found a bug where an agent threw away its own reasoning between turns. However, by the eleventh review, it had drifted back to small tweaks.

So the fix I'd build in is that nudge, on a schedule. Every two days or so, a cron job pastes the current board into the prompt with one blunt message: this is where everyone else is, we're in a local minimum, step back and think bigger.

The second fix is alerts, because the loop never told me when it was dead. For example, I set up an SSH tunnel through my MacBook so the AWS box could access the Harvard GPU cluster. In Assignment 5, a firewall rule on the AWS box broke it, and it stayed down for 119 hours. From 19 to 23 November, the worker ran zero GPU jobs, so every model swap in that window went straight to the leaderboard without a single local test. On 19 November, the worker's Claude login expired as well, and 19 hourly turns failed in a row. The funny part (sort of) is that my local Claude session even saw that expiry coming on day one. I just asked it to remind me, which of course it promised to do, but it never actually ended up doing so. The only "message" about this was the worker writing "still DOWN" into its state file every hour, which of course I didn't read (let's be honest here, who would...). Assignment 4 had the same problem: a fallback quietly shipped the wrong engine, and three submissions to the leaderboard ran the starter flags and tested nothing at all. A failed submission, a dead tunnel, an expired login, etc.: all of those should have been a notification to my phone or laptop, or an email, or anything that notifies me automatically so that I am able to fix the problem within the hour.

## Climbing a leaderboard is its own skill

This is more relevant to Assignment 3 than to 4 or 5, but in that assignment, I believe playing the leaderboard correctly was to some extent even more important than optimizing the harness to the best extent. Basically, I noticed that due to the model randomness, and due to the variance in turn speed (which I believe was because of different class utilization of the shared API), the same code submitted repeatedly scored very differently.

I think I played the leaderboard correctly by optimizing for one dimension and one dimension only, which was time per task solved. The way I did that was by implementing behaviors that, in my opinion, wouldn't generalize to a good harness in real-world applications, but worked well for pushing the Pareto front. The best example of this was a rule that just stopped the leaderboard attempt early, as soon as I had enough tasks solved to meet the baseline. Combine this with some harsh time and cost limits for when to drop an attempted task, and this naturally filters for easier tasks. Yet this wouldn't really help the quality of a harness in real-world applications; just imagine your harness stopping after completing some of the tasks you give it, only to be faster???

The more general point I want to make here goes beyond Assignment 3. Basically, I want to make you aware that you should know about the benchmarks you trust, understand how they are measured, and think about how they could be gamed by the frontier labs to create the illusion of a better harness (or rather model) than it actually is. The term benchmaxxing does not come from nowhere. There are plenty of examples of OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, etc. all playing this game, since every release basically HAS to beat the current Pareto front. Getting into detail here about all the different techniques those frontier AI labs are using to maximize their benchmark results goes beyond the scope of this post, but I'd recommend that the reader look into this. These articles could be useful starting points:
- [AI benchmarks: climbing the wrong hills](https://research.contrary.com/report/ai-benchmarks-climbing-the-wrong-hills) (Contrary Research): an overview of benchmaxxing
- [The Leaderboard Illusion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.20879) (Singh et al., 2025): private testing and selective reporting on Chatbot Arena
- [Meta's vanilla Maverick ranks below rivals](https://techcrunch.com/2025/04/11/metas-vanilla-maverick-ai-model-ranks-below-rivals-on-a-popular-chat-benchmark) (TechCrunch): 2nd place with a tuned variant, 32nd with the release model
- [OpenAI and FrontierMath](https://epoch.ai/latest/openai-and-frontiermath) (Epoch AI): the lab that funded the benchmark also had most of its answers
- [Google's Gemini and the MMLU comparison](https://spectrum.ieee.org/google-gemini) (IEEE Spectrum): comparing scores measured in different ways
- [Quantifying infrastructure noise in agentic coding evals](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/infrastructure-noise) (Anthropic): how the compute budget alone shifts Terminal-Bench scores
- [The SWE-Bench Illusion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.12286) (Liang et al., 2025): memorization in SWE-bench Verified


## AI use: yes!

In case it wasn't obvious by now: Claude did basically everything (coding, experiments, doing the uploads, planning, reviewing, etc., even helping me understand the assignments - it was basically involved in the entire process). It's hard to feel a sense of responsibility for this type of work when all I ever did was some slight steering (I am slightly exaggerating here), and even that, as said, not that much in Assignments 4 and 5 (although, to be fair, that's also where the subpar results come from). It almost hurts my ego knowing that a few years back I felt like I had skills other people didn't have, and learning how to code was a process that took time and work that I actually put in, but now seemingly everyone with access to Claude Code can just do it. The words "almost" and "seemingly" are doing a lot of work here, though, since the point I am trying to make is that the steering was what separated my high-ranking assignments from my low-ranking ones. And that one can only do with an understanding of the topic at hand, which (at least for now) helps me to sleep a bit better at night.
